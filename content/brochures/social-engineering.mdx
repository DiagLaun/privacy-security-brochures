## What social engineering really is

**Social engineering** is hacking people instead of computers. It does not need a vulnerability in your software. It only needs a moment when you are tired, distracted, or trying to be helpful.

Phishing is the best-known kind, but social engineering is broader. It includes:

- A "vendor" calling accounts payable and asking to update their bank info.
- A "new employee" walking in behind you through a badge-only door.
- A "support agent" texting that they need to "verify your account" by reading them a 2FA code.
- A "deepfake" voicemail from your boss asking for an urgent wire transfer.

The defenses are mostly habits, not tools.

## The six levers attackers pull

Most attempts use one or more of:

1. **Authority** — pretending to be a boss, executive, government agency, IT admin, or police.
2. **Urgency** — "This must happen in the next 30 minutes."
3. **Fear** — "Your account is being compromised right now." Or threats: legal action, account closure, embarrassment.
4. **Reciprocity** — small gifts or favors that create pressure to comply.
5. **Liking and trust** — building rapport over multiple messages before asking for the real thing.
6. **Social proof** — "Everyone in your department has already done this."

When you notice two or three of these levers stacking, something is off — even if the request itself sounds normal.

## The verify-out-of-band rule

The single most powerful habit: **for any sensitive request, verify through a different channel that you, not the sender, choose.**

If someone emails saying they're your CEO and asking for a wire transfer:

- Don't reply to the email.
- Don't call the number in the email signature.
- Call them on the number you already have for them, or walk over to their office, or message them on the company's chat tool.

This kills almost every business-email-compromise and impersonation attack at no cost.

## Common scenarios and how to handle them

### "IT support needs your password"

Real IT does not need your password. If they call you out of the blue, hang up and call your help desk through the number on your intranet.

### "We saw fraud on your account, read me the code we just sent"

Banks, exchanges, and tech support **never** ask you to read them a one-time code. Anyone asking is the attacker.

### "Quick favor — buy gift cards and send the codes"

Always a scam. No legitimate business asks employees to do this.

### "I'm at the door, my badge isn't working"

Tailgating into secure spaces is real. It is okay — and expected — to politely refuse and direct people to the front desk.

### "My number changed, save this new one"

A common precursor to a fraud message. Verify on the original number before saving anything.

### "Voice / video clip from a known person, asking for money or codes"

Deepfakes are good enough to fool you with three seconds of audio. Have a family or team **safe word**: a word only you know. If a request feels off, ask for the safe word.

## What to do today

- [ ] Pick a family safe word and share it with people who would ever wire or send money on your behalf.
- [ ] Decide your "verify channel" for work and for family — the channel you'll always use to confirm sensitive requests.
- [ ] Add a permanent rule: **no one will ever legitimately ask for my password or 2FA code**.
- [ ] Talk to your team or family about the gift card / urgent wire / "boss" patterns.
- [ ] Practice politely saying "Let me call you back through our normal channel."

> **Bottom line:** attackers want you to act now, on their channel. Your defense is to slow down, switch channels, and verify through someone or something you already trusted yesterday.
