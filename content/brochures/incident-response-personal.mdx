## Don't panic. Work the list.

In the first hour after you realize something has gone wrong, instinct is to freeze or to lash out by changing every password at once. Neither helps. What helps is a checklist you can run on autopilot, in roughly the right order. Print or save this one before you need it.

## Step 0 — Stop, breathe, and figure out what actually happened

Spend 60 seconds answering:

- **What was lost or compromised?** A device, a password, a code, a card, an account, a file?
- **Is it still happening?** A phishing site you typed into is different from a finished transaction.
- **Who else is affected?** Family, work colleagues, customers?

This shapes the rest of your response.

## Lost or stolen device

If a phone, laptop, or tablet was lost or stolen:

1. **Mark as lost** in Find My / find-my-device.
2. **Remote-lock** the device with a new code if it isn't already locked.
3. **If you have signs the device is gone for good, remote-wipe it.** Encryption + remote wipe makes the device useless to a thief.
4. **Sign out of sessions** — in your Google / Apple / Microsoft / Facebook / etc. account, revoke active sessions and trusted devices.
5. **Change the password** of accounts that were signed in on the device, starting with email.
6. **Replace SIM** if the phone has a physical SIM, to prevent SIM-based 2FA abuse. Add or strengthen your carrier account PIN.
7. **Report it** to local police, your employer, and your carrier as appropriate.

## Compromised email account

Email is the master key to everything that resets via "forgot password". Treat any compromise as urgent.

1. **Sign out of all sessions** and **change the password**. Use a generated one from your password manager.
2. **Turn on 2FA** if it wasn't already, ideally with a hardware key or authenticator app.
3. **Review forwarding and filters.** Attackers often add a rule that forwards a copy of every email to them, or auto-deletes password-reset emails.
4. **Review connected apps and OAuth grants.** Revoke anything you don't recognize.
5. **Check sent items, drafts, and recent activity** for messages you did not send.
6. **Reset passwords on dependent accounts** — banking, password manager, primary social, work — starting with the most sensitive.

## Compromised password (without phishing)

If a password manager or breach monitor tells you a password leaked, but you didn't type it on a fake site:

1. Change that password.
2. Change it everywhere you reused it (and stop reusing).
3. If the leaked account is high-value (email, bank, password manager), assume that account is partly compromised and run the full email recovery flow above.

## You typed a password on a phishing site

Same as above, plus:

1. Assume the attacker has your password **right now**. Speed matters.
2. Change the password and turn on 2FA before you do anything else.
3. Sign out of all sessions on that account.
4. If you also entered a 2FA code, the attacker may have a live session — sign-out and password change are essential.
5. Watch the account for the next week. Real-time monitoring beats periodic checking.

## You read a 2FA code to someone

This is the same as handing them a temporary password. Do exactly the same recovery as a phishing-site compromise, immediately.

## Card or banking fraud

1. **Lock or freeze the card** in the bank's app.
2. **Call the fraud line** on the back of the card or on the bank's official site. Not a number from a text or email.
3. **Dispute unauthorized transactions** — the timeline is usually short, so don't delay.
4. **Get a new card number.** A frozen card with the same number is still a leak.
5. **Check account-linked services** (subscription, ride-share, delivery apps) for changed payment methods.
6. **Review credit reports** (where available in your country) and consider a credit freeze if your identity, not just a card, is involved.

## Ransomware or suspicious software on a device

1. **Disconnect from the network.** Turn off Wi-Fi, unplug Ethernet.
2. **Do not pay** without consulting professionals. Payment doesn't reliably restore data and may be illegal in some jurisdictions.
3. **Take a photo** of any ransom note. Then power off.
4. **Restore from a clean backup** to a freshly imaged or factory-reset device.
5. **Assume credentials on the device were stolen** and rotate the most sensitive ones.

## After the immediate response

When things have calmed down, do the slower steps:

- File reports where required (employer, police, regulator, identity-theft service).
- Tell people who may be affected — friends and contacts who could receive scam messages from "you".
- Write down what happened, what worked, and what you'd change. The next incident is the time to be faster, not the time to learn.

## What to do today (before anything goes wrong)

- [ ] Save this brochure (or print it) somewhere you can find without your phone.
- [ ] Make sure Find My / find-my-device is enabled on every device.
- [ ] Make sure your email has 2FA — ideally a hardware key.
- [ ] Note your bank's fraud line; save it as a contact and check the card back.
- [ ] Make sure backups exist and you have tested a restore.

> **Bottom line:** in an incident, speed and order matter more than any single tool. Lock the device, secure email first, then work outward — and breathe.
