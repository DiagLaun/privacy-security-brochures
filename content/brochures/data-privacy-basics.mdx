## Privacy is about choice, not paranoia

You cannot stop every company from collecting data about you. That is not the goal. The goal is to know what is happening, choose what is worth sharing, and limit what you don't need to share.

Think of data privacy as personal hygiene rather than a one-time project. A few good habits cover most of what matters.

## Data minimization: give less, lose less

The single best privacy habit is **share less by default**. If a company never has a piece of your data, they can never leak it.

In practice that means:

- **Skip "register an account" when guest checkout works.**
- **Don't fill in optional fields.** Phone, birthday, address — required fields are marked. The rest is for marketing.
- **Use email aliases** (most providers offer them) so a leak from one site doesn't expose your main address.
- **Use a virtual or single-use card** for online shopping when your bank supports it.
- **Decline location, contacts, microphone, and camera access** unless an app actually needs them right now.

## Audit your big four

You don't have to fix everything. Spend an hour on these and you will be ahead of most people:

### 1. Your phone's app permissions

Settings → Privacy / Permissions. Look at which apps have:

- Location (especially "Always" — switch to "While using" or off)
- Microphone
- Contacts
- Photos (prefer "Selected Photos" over full library access)

Revoke anything that doesn't pass the smell test.

### 2. Your primary email account

In your inbox settings, check:

- Recent device sign-ins
- Forwarding rules (attackers often add a hidden forward)
- Connected third-party apps and OAuth grants — remove anything you don't recognize or no longer use.

### 3. Your social accounts

Make a single pass on each major account:

- Profile visibility — who can see your posts, friends, photos.
- Tagging settings.
- Old posts you would not post today.
- Connected apps and games — revoke unused ones.
- Ad personalization controls — most platforms let you turn this down.

### 4. Your browser and search

- Pick a search engine that suits you. DuckDuckGo, Brave Search, Kagi, and Startpage emphasize privacy.
- Sign out of long-running browser accounts you don't actively use.
- Clear or block third-party cookies (see *Safe Browsing*).

## Email aliases: the underused superpower

Aliases let you give every site a different address that all forward to your real inbox. Why this matters:

- You can tell which company leaked your address (it shows up in the "to" field).
- You can disable an alias when a site starts spamming you, with no effect on your other accounts.
- It hardens you against credential-stuffing attacks that rely on knowing your email.

Most major email providers, password managers, and dedicated services (SimpleLogin, Apple Hide My Email, Firefox Relay, DuckDuckGo Email Protection) offer aliases for free or cheap.

## Be skeptical of "free" services

If a service is free and it is not open-source or paid for by donations, your data is usually the product. That is not necessarily evil — but it changes how you should use the service:

- Don't connect it to your most important accounts.
- Don't give it more data than the feature actually requires.
- Read the data-deletion process before signing up. Some services make leaving harder than joining.

## Backups and the right to be forgotten

Privacy also means knowing your data **exists where you want it**.

- Back up the data you care about — photos, documents, password manager exports — to two places, one of them offline or off-site.
- For services you no longer use, **delete the account** rather than just abandoning it. An abandoned account is still a future breach waiting to involve you.

## What to do today

- [ ] Review app permissions on your phone. Turn off everything that fails the smell test.
- [ ] Set up an email alias for your next sign-up.
- [ ] Audit connected apps in your Google / Apple / Microsoft / Facebook account.
- [ ] Pick one old account you don't use and delete it.
- [ ] Decide on a search engine and set it as default.

> **Bottom line:** less data shared, fewer surprises later. A short audit of your phone, email, and social accounts gives you back more control than any single app or extension.
