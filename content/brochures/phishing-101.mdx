## What phishing actually is

Phishing is a scam that tricks you into handing over information — a password, a one-time code, a credit card, or your trust — by pretending to be someone you know or a service you use.

It works because it does not target your computer. It targets you, when you are tired, busy, or worried.

The medium changes. The pattern does not:

- **Email phishing** — fake login pages and urgent requests pretending to be your bank, employer, or a delivery service.
- **Smishing** — phishing via SMS or messaging apps.
- **Vishing** — phishing via phone calls, including AI-generated voice impersonation.
- **Spear phishing** — targeted at one person, often using real details about your job or life.

## Red flags you can train yourself to see

You do not need to be a security expert to catch most phishing. Look for these:

1. **Urgency or fear.** "Your account will be suspended in 1 hour." Real companies rarely act on those timelines.
2. **An unexpected link or attachment.** Especially zips, ISO files, or links to login pages from a notification you didn't expect.
3. **A mismatch between the sender and the link.** The sender shows "PayPal" but hovering the link reveals `paypal-secure-login.example.com`.
4. **Generic or off greetings.** "Dear Customer" from a bank that knows your name. Or your name spelled wrong.
5. **A request for a code, password, or remote access.** No legitimate support agent will ever ask you to read out a one-time code.
6. **Pressure to bypass normal channels.** "Don't call the front desk, just send the wire today."

## The 30-second check

When something feels off, slow down. Take 30 seconds and ask:

- Did I expect this?
- Who actually sent it? Look at the full email address, not just the display name.
- Where does the link go? On a desktop, hover. On mobile, long-press to preview the URL.
- Can I reach the sender through a channel I already trust? Not the number in the message — the number on the back of your card or the website you usually use.

If anything fails the check, do not click. Delete it or report it.

## What to do if you think you clicked

Do not panic. Do this in order:

1. **Disconnect the device** if a file was downloaded or you ran something. Turn off Wi-Fi.
2. **Change the password** of any account whose credentials you typed. Start with email — it controls password resets for everything else.
3. **Revoke active sessions** in the account's security settings.
4. **Turn on 2FA** if it isn't on.
5. **Check for unauthorized activity** — sent items in email, recent logins, unfamiliar rules or forwards.
6. **Tell someone** — your IT team at work, your bank's fraud line, or a trusted person. Reporting helps others.

## What to do today

- [ ] Add a sticky note to your screen: "Did I expect this? Verify the sender and the link."
- [ ] Practice hovering over links to preview URLs.
- [ ] Bookmark the real login pages of your top 5 accounts. Always go there directly.
- [ ] Tell your family the rule: no one will ever legitimately ask you for a one-time code.

> **Bottom line:** phishing wins on speed and emotion. Slow down, verify through a separate channel, and never trust a login page you arrived at through a link.
